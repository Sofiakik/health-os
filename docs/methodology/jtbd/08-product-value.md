# Product Value — How to Create It

## What Value Actually Is

**Value = deviation from expected value.**

Clients don't experience your product in a vacuum. They come with expectations about: cost, benefit, the job graph (how execution will unfold), and energy expenditure.

- **Positive deviation = WOW** — you delivered more than expected
- **Zero deviation = neutral** — you delivered exactly what was expected (clients feel nothing special)
- **Negative deviation = problem** — you delivered less than expected

This means you can create value in two ways: either by genuinely delivering more, or by **lowering expectations** so that the same delivery creates a WOW.

---

## Baseline Quality (Базовое качество)

**Baseline quality** is the expected level of execution that clients take for granted. Without it, clients consider the product insufficiently valuable — but achieving it earns zero gratitude, because it's assumed.

Examples:
- Airbnb: clean sheets, working wifi — expected, not WOW
- A doctor who doesn't misdiagnose — expected, not WOW
- A taxi that actually shows up — expected (mostly), not WOW

**Critical principle: What was WOW yesterday is baseline quality today.**

This is hedonic adaptation at the product level. Competitors' innovations raise the floor. What you shipped as a differentiator last year is now table stakes. Expectations only move in one direction: up.

Why this matters:
- Different segments have different baselines (a budget traveler's baseline ≠ a luxury traveler's)
- Baselines rise due to competitor actions you didn't choose
- Baselines rise naturally as people experience more value in all areas of life
- You must constantly track what your specific segment now considers "minimum"

---

## Interview Question to Uncover Expectations

To reveal what clients actually expect (not what they say they want):

> "By what criteria did you evaluate that you successfully got [expected result]?"

Or:

> "How did you want to get [expected result]?"

These questions expose the implicit standard the client was measuring against — not the feature wishlist, but the baseline they assumed would be met.

---

## Strategy: Correct Expectations to Create WOW

Instead of (or in addition to) improving your product, you can **lower expectations** so that your current product creates WOW.

Mechanism: solution → filters through client's expectations → perceived as WOW / neutral / problem

Practical applications:
- Be explicit about what the experience will involve (jobs, timeline, cost, quality) before the client starts — this shapes expectations before first contact
- Underpromise, overdeliver is the simplest version
- Communicate the job graph: show clients what steps to expect, how long each takes, what success looks like at each step

Example: ПРАВОВЕСТ Аудит (accounting/audit firm)
- Core Job: "Провести финаудит" (conduct financial audit)
- Big Job the client actually cares about: "Не потерять деньги на штрафах" (not lose money on fines)
- Their communication: "Guarantee protection from tax claims for 3 years in disputes with the tax authority. Financial insurance from Ingosstrakh for 30M rubles."
- This targets the Big Job level (fear of fines), not the Core Job level (audit process) — making the value immediately legible

## The 13 Mechanics of Creating Value

These are structured ways to create value within the JTBD framework. Use them to generate hypotheses about where to improve your product.

### 1. Start executing a job that isn't being executed at all
Someone has an important job. No solution exists. You solve it.

Example: After 2022 sanctions, Russians couldn't pay abroad. A fintech opened Kazakhstan accounts for Russian users — executing a job that had zero solutions.

### 2. Fix problems in job execution
The job has a solution, but it breaks at specific steps. Fix the broken steps.

Example: USDT crypto transfers on the ETH network were expensive and slow. Moving to the TRON network made them cheap and stable — fixing a problem in an existing job.

### 3. Fix non-executed jobs in a critical sequence
Some jobs in the sequence are being executed; others aren't. The whole chain suffers because of the broken links.

Example: Furniture store шкаф.ру — selection and ordering worked fine. But delivery and assembly were broken. The Big Job ("have a wardrobe in my home") couldn't be completed despite the early steps working.

### 4. Take job execution away from the person / automate it
The person is currently doing the job themselves. You do it for them, or eliminate the need to do it consciously.

Examples:
- Vkusvill: instead of figuring out what to cook (cognitively expensive), suggests ready meals based on what's in the fridge
- Sberbank: "transfer all" button for salary — eliminates the mental job of deciding allocation

### 5. Kill a job entirely
The job exists only because of a prior inefficiency. Remove the inefficiency; the job disappears.

Example: Mac hotspot connection with iPhone used to take 8 steps (find network, enter password, confirm, etc.). Now it takes 2 (device detects proximity, confirms automatically). Several jobs in the original flow were killed.

### 6. Reduce time gaps between jobs → faster Big Job completion
Each job takes time. Each gap between jobs takes time. Shrinking gaps accelerates the Big Job.

Example: Food delivery time went from 60 minutes to 25 minutes. The same jobs are executed, but the Big Job ("eat food I didn't cook") completes faster.

### 7. Execute more jobs with one solution
Consolidate multiple solutions for adjacent jobs into one, reducing friction and switching costs.

Example: Yandex GO (2024) consolidated three separate 2014-era apps — Delivery Club (food ordering), Vkusvill (grocery buying), Yandex Taxi (getting around the city) — into one product. The Big Job ("manage daily life logistics") now has one home.

### 8. Shift to another segment / add +1 segment at same job level
Same solution, different segment — differentiated by their version of the Core Job.

Example: Yandex Taxi operates three segments on the same job "get from A to B": Эконом (cheap), Комфорт (comfortable), Ultima (status + comfortable). Each segment has different expectations of execution quality and different willingness to pay.

### 9. Execute the next job in the critical sequence of Core Jobs
A client uses you for one job. The next job in their sequence currently has no good solution. You take it on.

Examples:
- Typeform: started with form creation. Added response analysis. Before: Typeform for forms + Google Sheets for analysis (2 tools, 2 interfaces, manual export). After: Typeform executes both.
- nomads.com: "choose where to nomad" (destination research). movingto.io: "get residency in a country" (visa/tax services). Two separate products covering adjacent jobs in the digital nomad's job sequence.

### 10. Give clarity on how the job graph looks — manage expectations
Show clients what the full job execution looks like: steps, quality, timeline, cost. This reduces anxiety and calibrates expectations, which increases perceived value.

Example: "Rent an apartment on your own" — 5 jobs, 2 weeks, 0 rubles cash, 20 hours of time. Making this explicit (before the client starts) sets expectations correctly and makes the solution's value immediately obvious by contrast.

### 11. Start executing one of the client's psychological needs
Jobs connect upward through life goals to fundamental psychological needs: Safety, Emotional Contact, Status, etc. A product that helps execute a job at the psychological need level creates deeper connection and is harder to replace.

Example: Buying a new apartment job ("выбрать новостройку") sits inside "Safety" (housing security), "Emotional Contact" (becoming a good husband/father), and "Status" (living in a good area). A product that speaks to these — not just to the functional selection task — creates stronger differentiation.

Example: Yandex Go post-ride tip screen with "compliment the driver" options — this is executing the psychological need for Emotional Contact, not the transportation job itself.

### 12. Correct expectations
Adjust client expectations (lower them) so that your actual delivery creates a WOW rather than a neutral.

Mechanism: The same product outcome is perceived differently depending on what the client expected going in. If you can shape expectations (via onboarding, marketing, sales conversation) before the experience, you can convert neutral deliveries into WOW moments.

### 13. Reduce Job/Solution Cost within one job without killing the job
Make the job cheaper, faster, or less effortful to execute — without removing the job itself.

Tags: create value, scale product, grow conversion. The ratio of Job/Solution Benefits to Job/Solution Cost improves.

### 14. Grow Job/Solution Benefits within one job
Increase the benefit delivered per unit of cost for the same job.

Tags: create value, retain clients, Go To Market, scale product.

## Important: Executing More Jobs ≠ Better

**"Выполнять больше работ != лучше" (executing more jobs is not the same as better)**

Adding more features/jobs to a product is only valuable if:
- The new jobs are critical in the sequence for your focus segment
- The client understands the value at the Core Job / Big Job level
- The jobs are for economically attractive segments

More jobs without strategic prioritization creates complexity that burdens users and dilutes competitive advantage.

---

## How to Rank Value Hypotheses

When you have multiple possible mechanics to apply, prioritize in this order:

1. **For economically more attractive segments** — focus your value creation where willingness to pay and segment size is highest
2. **For higher-level jobs** — the optimum is Big Job level and critical Core Jobs; criterion: does the client understand the value at CJ/BJ level?
3. **For starting to execute Big Job and critical sequences for new segments** — expanding by unlocking new segments through their Big Job is higher leverage than feature incrementalism

---

## The Value Growth Algorithm (Full Process)

1. **Hypothesize** which value creation mechanics can be applied to the job graphs of your focus segments
2. **Learn** the actual job graphs of focus segments — analytics + AJTBD research (qual → quant)
3. **Apply** mechanics to the information obtained
4. **Rank** mechanics using RICE (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort)

---

## Why This Framework > "Build Features and Fix Pain Points"

| Approach | Opportunity Cost |
|----------|-----------------|
| Build features & fix pain points | Unknown. In practice: enormous. You don't know what you're giving up. |
| Apply mechanics | Known and minimizable. You have a structured map of where value comes from. |

The mechanics framework forces you to understand which job level, which segment, and which chain link you're targeting — making the opportunity cost of each choice visible before you commit resources.

---

## The Core Principle

> "Не знаешь какую ценность создавать — в деталях изучай Big Job'ы твоих клиентов."
>
> Don't know what value to create? Study your clients' Big Jobs in detail.

The answer to "what should we build?" is almost always hiding in the full job graph of a specific, well-understood segment — not in feature requests or pain point lists.
