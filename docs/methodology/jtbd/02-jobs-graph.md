# The Jobs Graph

## What Is the Jobs Graph?

The **Jobs Graph** is a visual map showing how jobs relate to and connect with each other.

It transforms the job hierarchy into a structured diagram that reveals:
- What jobs are foundational (the "why")
- What jobs are tactical (the "how")
- How one job enables or leads to another
- Where opportunities and gaps exist

The graph moves from high-level aspirations at the top down to specific, concrete actions at the bottom.

## Structure of the Jobs Graph

The Jobs Graph typically flows vertically through distinct layers:

### Layer 1: Basic Needs (Top)
Fundamental human aspirations that anchor all subsequent jobs.

**Examples:**
- Have a home
- Have a career
- Be a good parent
- Be healthy

### Layer 2: Safety & Stability
Jobs that ensure the Basic Need is protected and secure.

### Layer 3: Emotional Connection
Jobs that connect to how we want to feel and be perceived.

### Layer 4: Actual Jobs
Concrete jobs that directly support the layers above.

### Layer 5: Specific Actions (Bottom)
The granular, tactical tasks that implement the jobs above.

## Example from Practice: "Have a Home"

Here's how a real job breaks down through the graph:

```
Basic Need (Top)
  ↓
"Have a home"
  ↓
Small Job
  ↓
"Buy an apartment"
  ↓
Core Jobs (What actually needs to happen)
  ├─ "Understand how to afford it"
  ├─ "Find where to buy"
  ├─ "Get mortgage approval"
  └─ "Pay mortgage"
  ↓
Specific Actions (Bottom)
  ├─ Research prices
  ├─ Calculate budget
  ├─ Find lender
  ├─ Prepare documents
  ├─ Submit application
  └─ Make monthly payments
```

Each level supports the one above it. You can't get mortgage approval without understanding how to afford it first. You can't afford it without researching prices.

## How to Read the Jobs Graph

### Vertical Relationships
The jobs above explain **why** the jobs below matter. The jobs below explain **how** to accomplish the jobs above.

- **Jobs above = Purpose**
- **Jobs below = Method**

### Horizontal Relationships
Jobs on the same level often represent:
- **Sequential steps** that happen in order
- **Parallel actions** that happen simultaneously
- **Alternative paths** to the same outcome

### What Each Node Represents
Each node in the graph is a job at a specific level of abstraction. Following the connections shows the complete journey a customer takes.

## Properties of Every Job

Beyond the hierarchy, every job has measurable properties that help you prioritize:

### Frequency
How often does this job come up?

- Daily, weekly, monthly, or annually
- Jobs that happen frequently are different from jobs that happen once every five years

### Importance
How critical is this job on a scale of 1-10?

- A job can be frequent but unimportant
- A job can be rare but absolutely critical

### Satisfaction
How well do current solutions fulfill this job on a scale of 1-10?

- "Very satisfied" = customer has a good solution already
- "Unsatisfied" = gap between what they need and what's available

### The Opportunity Formula
The biggest opportunities appear where:

**Importance is HIGH + Satisfaction is LOW**

These are jobs customers desperately need done but feel poorly served by existing solutions.

## How to Use the Jobs Graph in Practice

### Step 1: Map All Related Jobs
Start by listing every job your target customers have related to your product area. Don't filter yet — be comprehensive.

Include jobs at all levels: big aspirations, intermediate steps, and concrete actions.

### Step 2: Identify Your Product's Level
Determine which jobs in the graph your product actually competes on.

Are you solving a Big Job, a Small Job, or a Core Job? This matters because it shows what competitive landscape you're actually in.

### Step 3: Find the Opportunities
Score each job on:
- Importance (1-10)
- Satisfaction (1-10)
- Frequency

Look for jobs where importance is high but satisfaction is low. These are the gaps.

These gaps are where products win. Incumbents may own the high-satisfaction jobs. But the unsatisfied jobs? That's where you can establish new value.

### Step 4: Prioritize Your Focus
Not all jobs are worth solving. Use the graph to decide:

- Which unsatisfied jobs matter most to your customers?
- Which jobs come earliest in the sequence (solving early jobs enables later ones)?
- Which jobs create the most friction in the current solution?

## Previous and Following Jobs

Every Core Job sits in a sequence:

**Previous Job** → **Your Core Job** → **Following Job**

### Previous Job
The job that came before and triggered the need for your Core Job.

**Example:** If your Core Job is "Get mortgage approval," the previous job might be "Understand how to afford an apartment."

Understanding the previous job helps you:
- Know what customers already know or don't know
- Identify where they're coming from
- Create smooth transitions into your job

### Following Job
The job that comes after your Core Job and depends on having completed it.

**Example:** If your Core Job is "Get mortgage approval," the following job might be "Pay mortgage" or "Move into the apartment."

Understanding the following job helps you:
- See the full customer journey
- Ensure your solution sets them up for success in the next step
- Avoid solving your job in a way that makes the next job harder

## Using Context for Better Design

When you understand both the previous and following jobs, you design better solutions:

- You can anticipate what information or capability customers bring from the previous job
- You can design your solution to make the following job easier
- You can see opportunities to expand your product vertically (solving multiple jobs in the sequence)

A complete understanding of the job sequence is critical for product design that feels natural and integrated, not forced or isolated.

## The Jobs Graph as a Strategic Tool

The Jobs Graph is more than a diagram. It's a strategic decision-making tool that helps you:

1. **See the full landscape** — Where do you compete? What's adjacent?
2. **Find hidden opportunities** — Where do unsolved jobs exist?
3. **Understand your moat** — Which jobs do you own that others can't easily replicate?
4. **Plan your roadmap** — Which jobs should you solve next?
5. **Communicate your strategy** — Why are you working on *these* jobs, not others?

The graph keeps you honest. It reminds you that you're not building features — you're solving jobs.
