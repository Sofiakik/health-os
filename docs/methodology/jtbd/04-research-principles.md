# JTBD Research and Interview Principles

## Purpose of JTBD Research

JTBD research exists to answer one fundamental question: **Which jobs are real people actually trying to get done?**

Good JTBD research has three characteristics:

1. **Evidence-based:** You study what people actually did, not what they say they would do or what you predict they'll need.
2. **Focused:** Each interview moves you toward solving a specific business problem. You're not running general research — you're testing assumptions about a particular job or segment.
3. **Observable:** You study where actual energy and money were spent — the decisions people made when real consequences were at stake.

## Core Principles

### 1. Always study actual past behavior, not hypothetical future

**Study this:** "What did you do when... happened?"
- "What did you do the last time you needed to understand your health trends?"
- "Walk me through the last time you felt overwhelmed by all your health data."
- "Tell me about the last time you switched tools for tracking your health."

**Don't study this:** "What would you do if... happened?"
- "Would you use a tool that showed you correlations between sleep and recovery?"
- "What features would make you switch apps?"
- "How much would you pay for personalized insights?"

**Why:** People are notoriously bad at predicting their own behavior. When there are no real consequences, they'll tell you what sounds good, not what they'd actually do. Past behavior is the best predictor of future behavior.

### 2. The skill is knowing which jobs to study for this respondent

The questions you ask are almost always the same:
- "What were you using before?"
- "What triggered you to look for something new?"
- "What did you try first?"
- "What made you finally commit?"
- "What almost stopped you?"

**The skill is:** Given this respondent's context and goals, which 1-2 jobs should I go deep on?

Don't ask the same questions about every job. Map their job graph first, then choose the most important and most recent jobs to study deeply.

### 3. You can verify hypotheses, but always check if the respondent actually answered

You may go into an interview wanting to test a hypothesis: "I think customers need better performance metrics." That's fine.

**But:** Always verify that the respondent actually addressed that question, not just what you wanted to hear.

- Listen for whether they volunteered the answer or you led them there
- Ask follow-ups: "What made that important to you?"
- If they go vague, redirect: "Give me a specific example."
- Don't accept "That would be nice" — press on whether it's actually a job they're trying to get done

### 4. In B2B, always study personal jobs (not just company/team jobs)

Even in a business context, study the individual's personal jobs:
- "What does this mean for you personally?"
- "How does this affect your day-to-day work?"
- "If you solve this, what becomes possible for you?"

People buy and use tools based on personal jobs as much as company jobs. The person who can't look good to their boss, who can't manage their own time, or who can't reduce their stress — those are personal jobs that matter.

### 5. Problem interviews are a subset of JTBD interviews

Sometimes you need to diagnose a problem first: "Why aren't people using our product?"

Problem interviews focus on:
- What job were they trying to get done?
- What went wrong?
- How did they resolve it?
- What would have helped?

But problem interviews are still JTBD interviews. You're still studying jobs, just in the context of an unsolved problem or failed solution.

## Who to Interview

### Interview people who are actually trying to get the job done

The best interview subjects have one of these profiles:

**1. Active solution users (any solution in your space)**
- They're currently hiring something to get the job done
- They can tell you about their current struggles with existing solutions
- They can tell you what alternatives they considered and rejected
- Example: Interview someone using Whoop, MyFitnessPal, Oura, or other health tools

**2. Switchers (moved from one solution to another)**
- Gold. These are the most valuable interviews.
- They have direct experience with multiple solutions
- They experienced the moment when they decided one wasn't good enough
- They made a real, consequential decision
- Example: Someone who switched from Fitbit to Apple Watch to Whoop

**3. Churned users (tried and stopped)**
- They tried a solution and decided it didn't work
- They can tell you exactly where and why it failed
- They can tell you what they're using instead, or what they're doing without a solution
- Example: Someone who used a health app for 3 months and abandoned it

**Avoid:**
- People who don't use any solution in this space (no data on actual behavior)
- People who use your product but have never tried anything else (limited perspective)
- Casual browsers or non-users (they're not trying to get the job done)

## How to Select Which Jobs to Study in an Interview

### Start by mapping the job graph

Before going deep, understand the landscape:
- What jobs does this person try to get done related to health?
- Which are they actively hiring solutions for?
- Which matter most to them?
- Which are most recently in mind?

### Identify 1-2 jobs to go deep on

**Don't try to study everything in one interview.** You'll get surface-level answers to many questions instead of deep insight into the jobs that matter.

Choose:
- The most important job (highest consequence or frequency)
- The most recent job (top of mind, fresh memories)
- Or a job where they recently switched solutions (most valuable data)

### Go deep on the chosen jobs

Once you've chosen, study that job thoroughly:
- What triggered the need?
- What did they try first?
- Why did that not work?
- What made them finally find a solution?
- What almost stopped them?
- What emotions came up?

## Establishing Emotional Contact (Critical for Good Interviews)

The best interviews feel like a conversation between people who care about the same problem. The worst feel like interrogations.

Your goal is to create an environment where the respondent feels safe sharing real feelings and experiences, not just sanitized versions.

### Before the interview

**Research the respondent in advance**
- Look them up (LinkedIn, their writing, their social media)
- Find common ground (school, location, shared interests, mutual connections)
- Have one genuine compliment ready (not flattery, something real)

**Dress appropriately for the context**
- Match the environment you're walking into
- If you're interviewing a casual runner in their home, don't wear a three-piece suit
- If you're interviewing a high-powered executive, don't wear gym clothes
- Dressing appropriately says "I respect you and this conversation"

### During the interview

**Smile and give a genuine compliment early**
- Within the first 60 seconds, say something genuine and specific
- Not: "You seem nice" (generic)
- Yes: "I saw you finished that marathon last fall — that's incredible" (specific, shows you researched)
- This signals: I'm human, I'm interested in you, this is a conversation

**Show open body language**
- Lean slightly forward, not back
- Keep your hands visible, not crossed
- Make natural eye contact
- Sit at an angle (90 degrees), not directly facing across a table

**Active listen actively**
- Nod and say "yes," "aha," "I understand," "that makes sense"
- Don't just stay silent — this feels cold
- But don't interrupt or finish their sentences
- Let them lead, but show you're tracking

**Paraphrase their answers back to them**
- "So what I'm hearing is that you felt overwhelmed by all the data points, and you didn't know which ones actually mattered to you — is that right?"
- This does two things: confirms you understood, and gives them a chance to correct or clarify
- People feel heard when you paraphrase back

**Make small mistakes on purpose**
- "I thought health coaches always recommended 10,000 steps... that hasn't been your experience?"
- Or slightly misremember a detail: "So this was about 6 months ago...?"
- This makes you seem human, not like an expert interrogator
- It also gives them the chance to teach you, which feels good to most people

**Share your own emotions to normalize theirs**
- "That would frustrate me too"
- "I get anxious about that kind of thing"
- "That's something I've struggled with"
- If you show it's normal to have emotions, they'll share theirs

### Asking about emotions (critical)

Emotions are data. They signal what actually matters to the person.

**Set the expectation at the start**
- "I'm going to ask you some questions about how you felt in different moments — that okay?"
- This gives them permission and heads-up

**Demonstrate your own emotions first**
- Share a small emotional moment from your own life early in the interview
- This normalizes emotion and shows it's safe to share feelings here

**Ask for a 10-point scale when emotions are unclear**
- "On a scale of 1-10, how frustrated were you?"
- "How confident did you feel that this was the right solution?"
- Numbers make it easier and less awkward than open-ended emotion questions

**Don't hesitate to ask about negative emotions**
- "Were you angry? Ashamed? Confused?"
- "What made you feel stupid about it?"
- "How disappointed were you?"
- The tough emotions are often the most important data

**Cultural note:** Russians tend to use more cautious or restrained emotional language than Americans. Don't confuse linguistic restraint with lack of feeling. A Russian saying "it was frustrating" may be experiencing as much emotion as an American saying "I was furious." Listen for the underlying feeling, not the intensity of language.

## Common Mistakes in JTBD Interviews

### 1. Asking future/hypothetical questions

**Mistake:** "Would you use a tool that showed you correlations between sleep and recovery?"

**Why it's useless:** People will say yes if it sounds good. There are no consequences to "yes." You get no data about real behavior.

**Fix:** "Tell me about the last time you wanted to understand how sleep affected your recovery. What did you do?"

### 2. Asking leading questions that confirm your hypothesis

**Mistake:** "You probably care most about accuracy, right? That's what most people tell me."

**Why it doesn't work:** You're telling them what to say. They'll agree to be agreeable.

**Fix:** "What mattered most to you when you were looking for a solution?" (Then shut up and listen.)

### 3. Accepting generalizations

**Mistake:** "I usually check my data every morning" or "I'm always looking for ways to improve" or "We often have problems with data quality."

**Why it doesn't work:** "Usually," "always," "often" aren't data. People generalize when they haven't thought deeply. Push them to the specific instance.

**Fix:** "Walk me through the last time you checked your data. When was that? What specifically were you looking for?"

### 4. Taking opinions as insights

**Mistake:** "Consumers care about privacy." "People want real-time data." "Nobody likes complicated UIs."

**Why it's not data:** Opinions aren't behavior. They're what people think they should care about.

**Fix:** "Tell me about the last time privacy mattered to you in a health tool. What happened? What did you do?"

### 5. Not asking follow-up on emotions

**Mistake:** They mention an emotion in passing, and you move on.
- "Yeah, I was frustrated, but anyway, here's what I did next..."

**Why you miss it:** Emotions signal what actually mattered. Frustration is a clue. Shame is a clue. Fear is a clue.

**Fix:** "You mentioned being frustrated — tell me more about that. What specifically frustrated you?"

### 6. Studying too many jobs in one interview

**Mistake:** "Tell me about your whole health journey. Every tool you've ever used. Every goal you've had."

**Why you get weak data:** They'll give you summaries and highlights, not depth. An hour interview across 5 different jobs = 12 minutes per job = surface-level answers.

**Fix:** Identify 1-2 most important jobs. Go deep. Schedule another interview if you need to study more jobs.

## The Switch Interview Technique

**When a respondent has switched from one solution to another, you have the most valuable moment to study.** A switch represents a real decision made with real consequences. This is gold.

### The switch interview questions:

**"What were you using before?"**
- Get the full context of the previous solution
- How long did they use it?
- What was working and what wasn't?

**"What triggered you to look for something new?"**
- Don't ask "why did you want to switch" — too broad
- Ask what specific moment made them think "I need something different"
- This is the trigger moment — crucial data

**"What did you try first? Why?"**
- When they started looking, what was their first attempt?
- This shows their initial hypothesis about what they needed
- Why that and not others? (Reveals their evaluation criteria)

**"What made you finally commit to the new solution?"**
- What sealed the deal?
- What did it do that the others didn't?
- How did it feel to make the switch?

**"What almost stopped you from switching?"**
- Switching has friction: new learning curve, lost data, changed habits
- What almost kept them with the old solution?
- This reveals the strength of their commitment

**After the switch:**
- "How does the new solution compare to what you were using?"
- "What do you miss about the old one?"
- "What's better?"

## Checklist for Each Interview

Before you leave or end the call, verify you've covered the ground:

- [ ] **Gave a genuine compliment at the start** — did I make them feel seen?
- [ ] **Set expectation about emotion questions** — did I give them permission to share feelings?
- [ ] **Asked about actual past behavior, not hypothetical** — did I study what they DID, not what they WOULD do?
- [ ] **Got specific instances, not generalizations** — when they said "usually" or "often," did I push to a specific example?
- [ ] **Asked about negative emotions** — did I create space for frustration, confusion, embarrassment, fear?
- [ ] **Studied the trigger moment** — what made them feel the need? What was the moment it changed?
- [ ] **Studied previous and following jobs** — what were they trying to do before? What came after?
- [ ] **Asked about alternatives considered** — what else did they try or consider? Why did they reject it?

If you can check all of these, you'll have rich data about the actual jobs this person is trying to get done.

---

## Remember

The goal of JTBD research is not to validate your product idea. It's to understand the jobs people are actually trying to get done, so you can build something that solves them better than anything else available.

Listen for the job, not the feature. Listen for the emotion, not the summary. Listen for what actually happened, not what they think they should have wanted.
