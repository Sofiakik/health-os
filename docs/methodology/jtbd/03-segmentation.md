# Segmentation in Advanced JTBD

## Core Principle

**Everything for everyone = nothing for nobody.**

Segmentation in Jobs to Be Done is fundamentally about choice: **choosing which jobs, for which people, you will compete on**. You cannot serve every job equally well, so strategic segmentation is essential to building a focused, winning product.

## What Is a JTBD Segment?

A segment in JTBD is **NOT** defined by demographics alone.

The primary dimension is always the job: a specific group of people trying to accomplish a specific functional job. But demographics are not irrelevant — they appear in the **segment card format** alongside the job, not instead of it.

### Segment Card Format

A complete segment description combines both job and demographics:

```
Demographics: age, income, education, life situation
Когда (When/Context): the specific trigger situation
Хочу (Want): the job they want to execute
Чтобы (In order to): the ultimate outcome they're aiming for
Importance score: how critical this job is to this person
Current solution: what they use now
Problem with current solution: what's broken about it
```

There are two ways to write the context block:
- One large unified context block (if the job is always triggered by the same situation)
- Multiple separate Когда triggers (if the same job arises in different contexts for the same demographic)

The job + demographics combined format exists because two people with the same job may have wildly different willingness to pay, acquisition channels, and product needs based on their demographic context.

### The primary dimension is still the job

Two people of identical age, gender, and income can be in completely different JTBD segments if they're hiring a product to do different jobs. Conversely, people across very different demographics can be in the same segment if they're hiring a solution for the same job.

### Segmentation by similarity of job graphs

A more advanced method: group people not just by a single job, but by the **similarity of their entire job graph** — the pattern of which jobs they have, in what sequence, at what frequency. People with similar job graph patterns form the strongest natural segments because a product that serves one job graph step serves many of their other steps too.

## Segmentation as Strategic Choice

Choosing a segment means making three decisions simultaneously:

1. **Which job to compete on** — What core outcome are you solving for?
2. **Which people to serve** — Who is trying to get that job done?
3. **Resource allocation** — Given your capabilities and constraints, where can you win?

You're not dividing the market into slices. You're choosing which specific job-person pairing you'll dominate.

## How to Evaluate a Segment

Before committing resources to a segment, ask these questions:

### ROI: Can you create more value here than elsewhere?
- How underserved is this job currently?
- Are there solutions that are doing it well? (If the job is already excellently served, it's harder to compete.)
- How much better could you do it than existing alternatives?

### Opportunity cost: What do you give up by NOT choosing another segment?
- Which segments are you leaving on the table?
- Could those segments grow faster or have higher margins?
- Are you making an intentional choice, not just defaulting?

### Competition: How crowded is this space?
- How many competitors are already serving this job?
- Are new competitors entering?
- Can you differentiate meaningfully, or will you be one of many?

### Your ability to deliver: Do you have the resources to serve this job better than alternatives?
- Do you have the technical capability?
- Do you have the team expertise?
- Do you have distribution or brand advantage?
- Can you sustain better delivery over time?

## Example: The Taxi Industry

Consider how taxi/rideshare has multiple segments defined by different jobs:

**Segment A: Economy**
- Core job: "Get from A to B cheaply"
- Customer priorities: Low price, speed of arrival
- Features needed: Efficient routing, basic vehicle, minimal frills
- Example solution: UberX

**Segment B: Comfort**
- Core job: "Arrive relaxed and on time"
- Customer priorities: Reliability, cleanliness, professionalism
- Features needed: Good vehicle condition, professional driver, on-time guarantee
- Example solution: Standard taxi service with reputation

**Segment C: Premium/Status**
- Core job: "Make an impression / feel important"
- Customer priorities: Luxury, prestige, signal to others
- Features needed: High-end vehicle, impeccable service, exclusivity
- Example solution: Uber Black, Ultima

**These are the same product category** — moving people from one place to another. But they are **completely different segments** because the jobs are different, the customer psychology is different, and the features required are different.

A product trying to excel at all three simultaneously will excel at none.

## Finding the Right Core Job Level for Your Product

The granularity of your job definition matters enormously.

**Too broad:** You can't build a specific, differentiated solution.
**Too narrow:** You're defining a feature, not a job — the market may be too small.

### The Test

Ask yourself: **"Does my solution COMPLETELY fulfill this job, and do competitors NOT fulfill it as well?"**

- **If yes** → You've found the right Core Job level. You can build something competitors can't match.
- **If no** → You've either gone too broad (many things compete) or too narrow (your job is too specific). Adjust.

### Real Example: Health Analytics

Let's say you're building a health analytics product:

**Too broad:** "Stay healthy"
- Problem: Almost everything competes here — gyms, nutrition apps, doctors, books, wearables, supplements, etc.
- You can't win by doing "health" — it's too big.

**Too narrow:** "Log my HRV score after strength training on Tuesdays and show me the 8-week trend"
- Problem: This isn't a job — it's a feature. Too few people need this exact thing.
- And the market is essentially zero.

**Right level:** "Understand how my lifestyle choices affect my recovery and energy"
- Specific enough: You can build intelligence, personalization, and clarity that competitors don't provide.
- Broad enough: Dozens of people (maybe thousands) care about this job.
- Competitors don't fully solve it: Fitness apps don't connect recovery data to sleep and stress. Wearables don't explain the connections. This is a gap.

## Signs You Don't Know Your Segments

These are warning signs that your team hasn't done real segmentation work:

- You build and sell **features**, not jobs — your roadmap is a feature list, not a job list
- You don't know your product's **value** — you can't articulate what job it fully executes
- **Sales is hard** — prospects don't respond to your pitch because it doesn't map to their job
- **Clients don't understand value** — they use your product but can't explain why it's better than alternatives
- You're trying to **make everyone happy** — you keep adding things for different types of users with no unifying principle

> "You must abandon the naive and infantile position of making everyone happy — it's impossible to make everyone happy."

**Don't be afraid to reject segments.** Choosing a segment means explicitly deciding that other segments are not your priority. This is uncomfortable but necessary. A segment you reject today creates the focus that lets you dominate the segment you chose.

> "Choosing a segment = choosing your destiny." It defines your product roadmap, your sales motion, your marketing message, your hiring priorities, and your competitive positioning — all at once.

## Segment Profitability

Not all segments are economically equal. Before committing to a focus segment, assess structural profitability:

**Potentially profitable segments** have:
- Multiple jobs your product can execute (higher LTV)
- High importance/urgency of those jobs (higher willingness to pay)
- Jobs where execution failure is costly (pain is high)

**Structurally unprofitable segments** have:
- Too few jobs your product can help with (low LTV ceiling)
- Low importance of those jobs (low willingness to pay)
- Easy alternatives already available (switching cost is low)

Some segments **cannot in principle be profitable** given your product and unit economics, no matter how large they are. Identifying and rejecting these early saves enormous resource.

### Focus Segment

From the set of potentially profitable segments, select the **single most profitable segment** as your focus. This is the segment you:
- Build your core product for
- Write your primary sales scripts for
- Target your acquisition funnels at
- Use as the benchmark for all product decisions

You can serve other segments opportunistically, but the focus segment drives all primary product decisions.

**Real case — ARTDENT dental clinic:** By identifying and focusing on their highest-value segment (rather than treating all patients equally), they grew revenue from 38M to 53M rubles (+37%) and improved conversion from paid ads by +40%.

## Core Jobs: What Your Product "Practically Fully Executes"

A **Core Job** for your product is a job that your solution practically fully executes for the person — not just contributes to, but completes.

This is distinct from jobs where you play a partial role.

**Example — consulting for "exit employment":** The consultant practically fully executes the Core Job of "successfully leave my job and transition to independent work." They don't just advise — they guide, review contracts, handle negotiation frameworks, etc.

**Example — Биорг.Кадры (B2B HR):** Their Core Job for client companies is "hire the right employee without internal HR overhead." They execute the full recruitment sequence, not just one step of it.

**Example — Yandex Lavka:**
- Big Job: "feed myself and my family"
- Small Jobs within it: weekly grocery run, replace a missing ingredient, get ice cream on impulse, order a ready meal when cooking isn't happening
- Yandex Lavka's Core Jobs: the missing ingredient job and the ready meal job — not the full weekly grocery run (that's a different product), not ice cream only

The Core Job level question: does your product **completely fulfill** this job, such that the person doesn't need to go anywhere else for that specific outcome?

## Sequencing Your Market

Don't try to win all segments at once. Smart products sequence their entry:

### The typical sequence:

1. **Win one specific subsegment deeply first**
   - Choose the subsegment where you can deliver the most value relative to competition
   - Target ~200M rubles revenue potential at this level
   - Build your acquisition funnels, sales scripts, and product for this one subsegment only
   - Become the obvious choice in that niche; build reputation, word-of-mouth, case studies

2. **Expand to adjacent subsegments within the same segment**
   - Your product, positioning, and team learnings transfer
   - You now have proof points and credibility
   - Adjacent subsegments often have similar jobs with similar solutions
   - Total addressable revenue within the full segment: ~2B rubles

3. **Scale the business through systematic subsegment expansion**
   - Create value in the focus subsegment → retain and grow → drive reorders
   - Then expand to the next adjacent subsegment with the proven model
   - Repeat systematically — this is how Yandex.Taxi expanded across city after city and category after category

**The Yandex.Taxi growth pattern:** Started in one city, one service type (economy). Then systematically added city after city, then service type after service type (comfort, premium, cargo, food), using the same expansion logic each time.

**Why this works:**
- You're not diluting effort across every possible market
- You're building momentum and credibility in one place first
- You can refine your product and positioning based on real data
- By the time you expand, you have proof that your approach works

## For Health OS: Questions to Explore

As you think about segmentation for Health OS, consider:

### Who is the customer?
- Who currently "hires" a solution to understand their health data?
- Fitness enthusiasts? Health-conscious professionals? Athletes? Patients with chronic conditions? All of the above?

### What job are they hiring for?
- Is it "maximize athletic performance"?
- Is it "manage a chronic health condition"?
- Is it "understand how daily habits affect my energy"?
- Is it "prove to my doctor that I'm taking care of my health"?
- Different people, different jobs.

### Which jobs are underserved?
- Which jobs matter to people but current solutions don't fulfill well?
- Where is there high importance but low satisfaction?

### Where can you win?
- Which segment can you serve better than anyone else with your current product, team, and resources?
- This is not "which segment is biggest" — it's "where can we be the best?"

Segmentation is not about predicting the future market. It's about choosing where to compete based on your competitive advantage and the jobs people are actually trying to get done.
