# B2B JTBD Interview Guide

A practical reference for conducting Jobs-to-be-Done interviews with business users and decision-makers.

---

## Key Differences: B2B vs B2C

Understanding these differences is essential for B2B research.

### Multiple Stakeholders with Different Jobs

- There is rarely one buyer, one user, and one decision-maker
- Each stakeholder has a **personal job** that may not align with the company's business objectives
- The same product might solve different jobs for different people

### Always Study Personal Jobs

- B2B buying decisions are ultimately made by people, not companies
- Study the **personal jobs** of each stakeholder: career advancement, looking good in front of their manager, avoiding risk, reducing stress, proving their worth
- Company/team jobs matter, but personal jobs often outweigh them in determining decisions
- When founders are not actively involved, personal motivation can be 90% of the decision

### Long B2B Buying Cycle

- Median B2B sales cycle: 141 days across 4 distinct stages
- Multiple stakeholders must align before purchase
- What triggers the need for one person might take weeks to reach another
- Understanding the buying timeline is key to understanding when each job becomes active

### Buyer ≠ User ≠ Decision-Maker

- **The Buyer** might never use the product (procurement person)
- **The User** might not have wanted to buy it (end user dealing with imposed solution)
- **The Decision-Maker** might care most about budget and risk, not actual job fit
- You must interview all three roles to understand the full picture

---

## The 7 Stakeholder Roles in B2B

For each role, understand their **personal job**, not just their business function.

### 1. Initiator

**Who:** The person who first identified the need or problem

**Their personal job:** Often proving they're strategic, proactive, or visionary. May want credit for solving a problem.

**What to ask:** "What made you realize [problem] needed to be solved? What would happen if it stayed unsolved?"

---

### 2. User

**Who:** The person who actually uses the product every day

**Their personal job:** Making their own work easier, faster, or better. Often avoiding embarrassment or looking incompetent. May have concerns about job security if automation is involved.

**What to ask:** "How would this change your day-to-day work? What would you like about it? What would concern you?"

---

### 3. Influencer

**Who:** Shapes the decision without having final approval authority

**Their personal job:** Being seen as knowledgeable and influential. Wanting to be consulted and have their opinion matter.

**What to ask:** "What would make you confident recommending this? What would concern you?"

---

### 4. Decision-Maker

**Who:** Has final approval authority

**Their personal job:** Making sound business decisions, minimizing risk, staying within budget, making their boss happy, avoiding looking foolish.

**What to ask:** "What matters most to you in evaluating solutions like this? What would make you hesitant?"

---

### 5. Buyer

**Who:** Handles procurement and payment

**Their personal job:** Completing the purchase smoothly, following process, negotiating good terms, not wasting time on lengthy vendor evaluations.

**What to ask:** "What's your process for evaluating and approving purchases in this category?"

---

### 6. Gatekeeper

**Who:** Controls access to decision-makers

**Their personal job:** Protecting their boss's time, filtering out bad ideas, being seen as a good filter.

**What to ask:** "What would make you think this is worth your boss's time?"

---

### 7. Champion

**Who:** Internal advocate who drives the purchase forward

**Their personal job:** Solving a real problem they care about, being the person who "made it happen," advancing their career through successful projects.

**What to ask:** "Why do you personally care about solving this? What would winning look like for you?"

## B2B Interview Structure (6 Phases)

**Total time:** 50-90 minutes depending on complexity

---

## Phase 1 — Introduction (3-5 minutes)

**Goal:** Establish rapport and set expectations

### Script

Same as B2C guide:

- Thank them for their time
- Explain briefly what you'll discuss (you can mention the job hypothesis but not business details)
- Tell them you'll take notes
- Warn that you'll ask about personal impact — explain why it matters for good business decisions
- Ask permission to record
- Tell them to ask if any question is unclear or uncomfortable

---

## Phase 2 — Context Discovery (8-10 minutes)

**Goal:** Understand their role, responsibilities, and how they currently approach [job area]

### Key Questions

**"Please tell me briefly about your role and what you're responsible for."**

**What to listen for:** Their title, who they report to, what success looks like for them, what keeps them up at night.

---

**"How do you currently handle [job area]?"**

**What to listen for:** Their existing process, tools, workarounds, who helps them, how much time it takes, what frustrates them.

---

**"Who else is involved in decisions about [job area]?"**

**What to listen for:** Map the stakeholder ecosystem. Who influences them? Who do they have to convince? Who might object?

---

**"How long have you been doing this role / working with [current solution]?"**

**What to listen for:** Their experience level and how entrenched they are in the current way.

---

## Phase 3 — Value & Importance (8-12 minutes)

**Goal:** Understand what outcomes matter to this person personally

### Key Questions

**"What result are you trying to get from [solution]?"**

**What to listen for:** The concrete outcome. Push past vague answers like "improve efficiency" — get specifics.

---

**"Why does that result matter to you personally?"**

**Important:** This is not asking why it matters to the company. It's asking what's in it for them. What does it do for their career, their credibility, their workload, their stress level?

**What to listen for:** The real motivation. Examples: "My manager would see me as more strategic," "I wouldn't have to stay late manually fixing data," "I'd have proof that my team is performing."

---

**"What happens to you if you don't get that result?"**

**What to listen for:** The negative consequence for them personally. Not "the company loses money" but "I look unprepared in meetings" or "my team blames me" or "I can't take on the projects I want."

---

## Phase 4 — Job Articulation (20-30 minutes)

**Goal:** Define the job precisely through their eyes

Use the same question blocks as the B2C guide, but with B2B framing. Focus on personal career, status, and safety motivations, not just company goals.

### Full Question Set

#### [Brief Story]

"Please tell me how you currently handle [job area] — what solutions do you use and how do you buy them?"

**What to listen for:** The full current-state process and any pain points with existing solutions.

---

#### [Expected Result]

"What outcomes do you expect from [solution]? What does success look like for you personally?"

**What to listen for:** Their success metrics, not the company's. "I'd spend less time in meetings," not "reduce meeting time by 30%."

---

#### [Navigation to Jobs — if multiple jobs mentioned]

"Of everything you mentioned, which is most important to you personally? Most problematic? Most frequent?"

**What to listen for:** Prioritize which jobs to study in depth.

---

#### [Trigger]

"When do you typically start looking for a solution to [job]? What sets it off?"

**What to listen for:** The moment when this job becomes urgent. Is it event-driven (quarterly review, new mandate)? Accumulating frustration?

---

#### [Value]

"What is the value of solving [job] well — for the company, and for you personally?"

**What to listen for:** Get both answers. They may diverge significantly.

---

#### [Importance Scale]

"On a scale of 1-10, how important is solving [job] for you right now?"

If they answer 8-10:
- "Help me understand why it ranks so high for you personally?"

**What to listen for:** What's driving the urgency? Is this a new crisis, or an ongoing frustration that's reached a tipping point?

---

#### [Criteria]

"What would an ideal solution look like? What criteria matter most?"

Examples: ease of use, speed, security, compliance, visibility/reporting, training required, implementation time, vendor stability, support.

**What to listen for:** Ask follow-ups: "If you could only have three of those, which three would you keep?" This reveals what truly matters.

---

#### [Brief Job Description]

"If you were to describe what you're trying to accomplish in one sentence, what would you say?"

**What to listen for:** The language they use. Capture it exactly — it will help you communicate with similar personas.

---

#### [Negative Emotions / Barriers]

"What concerns do you have? What would hold you back from moving forward?"

"What would your biggest objection be?"

**What to listen for:** Risk aversion, skepticism, change fatigue, political concerns, fear of looking foolish.

---

#### [Activating Knowledge] (optional)

"Was there a moment or experience that made this problem feel urgent?"

**What to listen for:** The event that changed this from "nice to have" to "need to solve." A failed project? A reorganization? New competition?

---

## Phase 5 — Outcome & Criteria (6-8 minutes)

**Goal:** Understand success metrics and what would make them look good

### Key Questions

**"How would you know this is working?"**

**What to listen for:** Concrete metrics they could point to. "I'd see a 20% reduction in…" or "My manager would stop asking me about…" or "I'd have time to work on…"

---

**"What would make you look good in front of your team/manager?"**

**What to listen for:** The career/status dimension. What would prove their value or judgment?

---

**"What would make you look bad?"**

**What to listen for:** The downside risk they're worried about. Implementation failure? Wasted budget? Team adoption challenges?

---

## Phase 6 — Emotional Barriers (6-10 minutes)

**Goal:** Understand what stops B2B decisions from happening

### Key Questions

**"What concerns do you have about changing your current approach?"**

**What to listen for:** Risk factors, switching costs, emotional attachment to the status quo, fear of the unknown.

---

**"What would make you hesitant to recommend this to your team/boss?"**

**What to listen for:** Political barriers. Who might object? What are their likely objections? What would it take to convince them?

---

**"What would your manager's objections likely be?"**

**What to listen for:** The layer above them. Often the real barriers exist at the next level up, not with the person you're talking to.

---

**"Is there anything else that concerns you about making this change?"**

**What to listen for:** Catch-all for risks they haven't explicitly mentioned.

---

## The B2B Switching Moment (Most Valuable Signal)

When a company switches from one solution to another, you get a clear signal of what job became important enough to override inertia and switching costs.

### Switching Interview Questions

**"What triggered the search for something new?"**

---

**"Who initiated the switch? What made them decide to look?"**

---

**"Walk me through the internal process — who got involved, how long did it take, what almost stopped it?"**

---

**"What was almost a dealbreaker? What almost stopped the switch from happening?"**

---

**"Who had to be convinced, and what actually convinced them?"**

---

**"What did you miss about the old solution once you switched?"**

**What to listen for:** This reveals what the old solution did well, so you understand the tradeoffs they made.

## Common B2B Research Mistakes

Avoid these to get better data:

### Mistake 1: Only One Stakeholder

Interviewing only the user and assuming they represent the whole decision. You miss that the decision-maker cares most about cost, or that the initiator has political constraints.

**Fix:** Map all stakeholders and interview at least the user and one decision-maker.

---

### Mistake 2: Asking About Company Needs Without Personal Motivation

A user will tell you what they think they should care about ("improve team efficiency") rather than what they actually care about ("I don't want to be blamed when reports are late").

**Fix:** Always ask "why does this matter to you personally?" and push for honest answers about career, status, and risk.

---

### Mistake 3: Treating the Stated Business Reason as the Real Reason

The company approved the budget for "enterprise-wide automation," but the real driver might be "CEO is unhappy with manual processes and someone needs to fix it quickly."

**Fix:** Ask why this is urgent now. What changed? What would happen if it wasn't solved?

---

### Mistake 4: Not Asking About Personal Consequences

"What happens to you if we don't solve this?" reveals whether this is their problem or just their job. If nothing personally bad happens, it won't get prioritized.

**Fix:** Always ask the personal consequence question.

---

### Mistake 5: Assuming the Initiator is the Decision-Maker

The person who identified the problem often has low decision authority. The decision-maker might not even know there's a problem yet.

**Fix:** Ask "Who would need to approve a solution?" and interview that person.

## After the B2B Interview — What to Capture

### Stakeholder Role & Context

- Which role were you talking to? (user, decision-maker, initiator, etc.)
- What's their title and who do they report to?
- How long have they been in this role?

---

### Their Personal Job (Most Important)

Document it as:
> When [situation], I want to [outcome], so I can [personal benefit]

Example: "When our quarterly reports are due, I want to have clean data with no manual fixes, so I can present to my CFO without looking unprepared."

---

### Importance & Satisfaction Scores

- Importance (1-10): How important is solving this job right now?
- Satisfaction (1-10): How satisfied are they with current solutions?
- If either score is 8+, dig deeper

---

### Stakeholder Map

List:
- Who else is involved in the decision?
- Who influences this person?
- Who would this person need to convince?
- Who might object, and what are their likely jobs?

---

### Political Dynamics

- Who has the real power in this decision?
- Are there hidden agendas or conflicts?
- What would get a "no" despite surface-level agreement?
- Who benefits from the status quo?

---

### Switching Trigger (if relevant)

- What would make them switch from their current solution?
- What's the activation threshold?
- How long can they tolerate the current pain before they move?

---

### Emotional Language

Capture their exact words when talking about:
- Success: "What words do they use?"
- Failure: "What's their frustration tone?"
- Fear: "What specifically do they worry about?"

---

## Interview Tips for B2B

**Remember: you're talking to a person in a job, not a job title.** The CFO isn't going to use this product. A person trying to keep their CFO happy will.

**Uncover the unspoken job.** What don't they want to admit matters? Often it's status, politics, or fear of looking bad.

**Ask about emotions first, problems second.** The emotions reveal the motivation; the problems reveal where solutions fail.

**Map the ecosystem.** Every person you talk to should make you smarter about who else you need to talk to.

**Watch for answers that are too polished.** When someone gives you their "official answer," that's when you need to push: "But what about your personal experience with this?"

**Listen for disagreement.** When someone says "But my boss wants..." that's a signal their job conflicts with someone else's job. That's valuable.

**Don't pitch.** B2B people are used to salespeople. If they think you're selling, they'll give you sales answers, not truthful ones. Stay curious and neutral.
